### Proposed paragraph: after the premium bridge

A facility should first be priced as a sequence of money amounts. The lender projects interest and fees it expects to receive, the cost of funding the balance and commitment, servicing expense and expected credit loss. It then compares the remaining contribution with the capital allocated to the risk. Converting those amounts to a spread requires a stated balance and time basis. Keeping this calculation beside the borrower's repayment calendar exposes two different weaknesses: a facility can fail the lender's return requirement, or its payment dates can exceed the borrower's cash capacity. Improving one does not establish the other.

### Proposed paragraph: after collection timing

The same recovery percentage can produce different funding needs. A claim expected to settle in six months does not meet an instalment due tomorrow, and cash in a restricted account may not be available to the entity that needs it. The collection forecast should therefore identify amount, expected receipt date, settlement status, restrictions and allocation. Treasury can then distinguish a change in ultimate loss from a change in the time and cost of carrying the exposure.

### Proposed paragraph: after the SPV overview

Institutional funding begins with a receivable whose rights and cash history can be reconstructed. The vehicle model follows eligibility, purchase, servicing, collections and distributions under the transaction documents. A trigger is useful only when the model also shows the cash action it causes: stopping purchases, curing a reserve, redirecting collections or amortising notes. The resulting investor report should reconcile to both the asset tape and the controlled accounts.

## Three initial article briefs

### 1. A loan has two cash calendars

**Question:** How can a facility appear attractive to the lender while missing the borrower's operating cycle—or repay reliably while earning an inadequate lender return?

Follow one MSME order from materials through customer collection and debt service. Compare term and revolving funding. Show the borrower cash calendar beside lender contribution and funding use. Add delayed receipts and an unused commitment. End with the decisions that can change: amount, tenor, repayment pattern, fee, funding source or whether to lend. Link to Owner Earnings Parts 3–4 and Making Business Credit Work. Suggested exhibit: two aligned weekly cash schedules and one pricing bridge.

### 2. A guarantee has a payment date

**Question:** What does protection deliver between default and settled cash?

Follow an eligible loan into a claim, including documentation, exclusion checks, settlement delay, counterparty performance and recovery sharing. Compare no protection, a delayed guarantee and funded support using equal underlying borrower cash flows. Explain the difference between reduced loss and a funded liquidity gap. Suggested exhibit: claim timeline, gross-to-net loss and cash-gap bridge. Include borrower continuity and servicing responsibilities.

### 3. When a receivable is ready for institutional funding

**Question:** What makes a pool financeable beyond a favourable default forecast?

Move from verifiable assets and transfer rights to eligibility, servicing, borrowing base, reserves, waterfall and triggers. Use one normal month and one disrupted month. Explain which cash is available to which party and what happens when a trigger activates. Suggested exhibit: reconciled payment-date report and trigger-driven changes in note cash. Connect to Mwendo's transaction-specific model.
